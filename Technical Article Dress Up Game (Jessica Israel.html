a field note from the studio

Threads of Code

We set out to build a browser-based dress-up studio with nothing but JavaScript and SVG, and somehow ended up with an hourglass figure, six kinds of pants, four pairs of shoes, and a face cute enough for a mobile game. Trial and error included, generously.

By Jessica Israel

If you've ever lost an entire afternoon to a character creator (cycling through hairstyles, testing fifteen outfit combos, agonizing over whether the boots or the heels look better), you already understand the appeal of this project better than most tutorials will admit. That itch to just see one more look is exactly what we built here: a browser-based dress-up studio where you can mix and match hair, outfits, skin tone, body type, and makeup, and watch her transform instantly.

The best part? You don't need Photoshop, a stack of downloaded sprites, or any art skills at all. Everything on screen (her body, her clothes, her sparkly little eyes) is drawn straight from code. Change a few values, and the whole look shifts in real time. Here's how it came together, mess-ups included.

1

Sketching a Silhouette, Not a Shape

Every character has to start somewhere, and ours started as a single rounded rectangle standing in for a torso. It got the job done in the sense that clothes could technically hang on it, but there was no waist, no hip, no shape, just a blocky outline with a head balanced on top.

The fix was drawing an actual outline instead: one continuous shape that curves in at the waist and swells out gently at the hip, the way a real figure reads as a body instead of a placeholder:

const torsoPath = "M100 118 Q90 129 94 140 Q88 158 106 175 
                   Q100 190 100 205 Q130 214 160 205 
                   Q160 190 154 175 Q172 158 166 140 
                   Q170 129 160 118 Q145 108 130 114 
                   Q115 108 100 118 Z";

That wall of letters looks intimidating, but really it's just a trail of breadcrumbs: go here, curve toward this point, go there, curve again. Follow the whole trail around in a loop and you've traced a shoulder, a waist, a hip, with no drawing tablet required.

2

Learn That Order Is Everything

Here's a twist that catches almost everyone off guard the first time: you can build every single piece of an outfit perfectly, and the character can still look completely broken, because of the order you stacked things in.

At one point, the torso was accidentally being drawn after the skirt. Every shape was correct. And yet the skirt kept vanishing, buried under a fresh layer of skin painted right on top of it. It's the digital version of putting your shirt on before your jacket and then wondering where the jacket went.

The fix was locking in a strict backstage order: a lineup every layer has to follow, no cutting in line:

// legs → torso → bottoms → tops → head → hair → makeup → shoes

Once that running order was set, everything clicked into place. Turns out what you draw and when you draw it are two totally different questions, and the second one decides what actually shows up on screen.

3

Build an Actual Closet

A dress-up game lives or dies by its wardrobe, so the goal was real variety, not just the same shape recolored five times. The bottoms alone grew into six genuinely distinct pieces: a pleated skirt, a mini, a floor-sweeping maxi, wide-leg trousers, skinny jeans, and flared denim, each one shaped to actually hug her waist instead of floating over it like a sticker slapped on top.

function applyChoice(category, name) {
  current[category] = name;
  renderCharacter();
}

That's genuinely most of the interaction. Tap "Maxi Skirt," and her outfit redraws itself instantly: no reload, no flicker, just a costume change that happens faster than you can blink.

Then came a request every dress-up fan eventually makes: what about dresses? A Dresses tab was added that swaps in one-piece looks (slip, wrap, A-line, maxi) and automatically benches the separate top and bottom the moment one is picked, so nothing pokes out underneath by mistake:

function renderCharacter() {
  const wearingDress = current.dress !== "None";
  document.getElementById('top').setAttribute('d', 
    wearingDress ? "" : paths.top[current.top]);
  document.getElementById('bottom').setAttribute('d', 
    wearingDress ? "" : paths.bottom[current.bottom]);
  document.getElementById('dress').setAttribute('d', 
    wearingDress ? paths.dress[current.dress] : "");
}

That little wearingDress check is basically a bouncer at the door, deciding who gets to show up and who has to sit this one out.

4

Shoes That Aren't Just Colored Blobs

For a while, "four shoe options" was a small lie: it was really one shoe shape wearing four different coats of paint. A boot and a stiletto heel had the exact same silhouette, just tinted black or nude. Nobody was fooled, least of all us.

The real fix meant giving every shoe its own actual shape, with color and style bundled together as a matching set:

shoe: {
  "Black Boots": { color: "#000000", d: "M100 280 H128 V310..." },
  "Nude Heels":  { color: "#d9a679", d: "M104 300 Q98 306..." },
  "Red Flats":   { color: "#c23b4a", d: "M98 312 Q94 318..." },
  "White Sneakers": { color: "#f4f4f4", d: "M96 306 Q92 312..." }
}

Now a boot has an actual shaft and sole. A heel has a real heel. And since they're painted onto her legs before the pants go on, hemlines drape over the boot shaft the way they're supposed to, instead of the boots awkwardly floating in front of everything.

5

Let Her Actually Look Like Someone

Clothes are only half of what makes a character creator satisfying: the rest is being able to see yourself, or someone like you, in the result. A Skin tab offers a real range of tones instead of a single default. Makeup adds blush, lip color, and fluttery lashes, all placed carefully enough that nothing ever creeps over the eyes or disappears under her hair.

Then there's Body type (slim, average, curvy, plus), which sounds like it should mean drawing four totally different people from scratch. Instead, it's one clever shortcut:

const scale = bodyScales[current.bodyType];
bodyGroup.setAttribute('transform', 
  `translate(130,0) scale(${scale},1) translate(-130,0)`);

That single line stretches her whole frame sideways around her center, and because her outfits live inside that same group, they stretch right along with her. Switch to "Curvy" and her jeans don't gap open at the waist; they resize in step, like the clothes actually know whose body they're on.

Her face got its own glow-up too, trading tiny plain dot eyes for big glossy ones with double sparkle highlights, and swapping a wide smile for something smaller and softer, landing her right in kawaii territory.

The Real Lesson Hiding Inside a Dress-Up Game

Strip away the outfits and the sparkly eyes, and what's left is a genuinely useful set of habits for building anything visual: draw things in the right order, look for the one clever shortcut that saves you from repeating yourself, and keep a single running list of "what's currently selected" so every piece (body, face, hair, outfit, shoes) stays in sync instead of drifting into its own little world.

Turns out getting a character dressed and getting a program "dressed" aren't so different after all. It's all about layers, in the right order, fitting the body underneath.

Cheat Sheet: The Code, Explained

A plain-English breakdown of every piece of code above: what it does, why it's written that way, and how the pieces snap together.

SVG path commands: the alphabet of drawing

Every shape in this game is a single <path> element with a d attribute: a string that tells the browser how to draw a line. Think of it as turn-by-turn directions for an invisible pen:

CommandMeansExample
M x yMove the pen without drawing (the start)M100 118
L x yDraw a straight line to this pointL160 260
H xStraight horizontal line to this xH72
V yStraight vertical line to this yV310
Q cx cy, x yCurve, bending toward the control pointQ90 129 94 140
ZClose the path, back to the startZ

Everything curvy in this game (the waist, the sleeves, the hair) comes from stacking Q commands one after another. The control point is like a magnet pulling the line toward it without the line actually touching it.

The current object: the character's memory

let current = {
  top: "Crop Blazer",
  bottom: "Pleated Skirt",
  dress: "None",
  hair: "Long Waves",
  shoe: "Black Boots",
  skinTone: "Light",
  makeup: "None",
  bodyType: "Average"
};

This one object is the entire "save file" for her current look. Every button click updates one property on it, and then a single function, renderCharacter(), reads the whole object and redraws everything to match. Nothing gets drawn directly by a button; the button just updates the memory, and the render function does the actual work. This update-state-then-re-render pattern is the same idea behind almost every modern web app, from to-do lists to a social media feed.

The paths dictionary: the closet itself

const paths = {
  top: {
    "Crop Blazer": "M94 120 Q78 135...",
    "Puff Sleeve": "M100 115 Q70 118...",
  },
  bottom: { /* six more entries */ },
  shoe: { /* shape + color pairs */ }
};

A big lookup table: category → outfit name → the actual path string (or, for shoes, a shape and a color together). Tap "Puff Sleeve," and the code fetches paths.top["Puff Sleeve"] and hands it to the shape on screen.

getElementById and setAttribute: the paintbrush

document.getElementById('top').setAttribute('d', paths.top[current.top]);

Translated: "Find the shape on the page named top, and repaint its outline using whatever shape is currently selected." This one line runs every time you change outfits; it's the moment the drawing actually updates.

Conditional rendering: the bouncer pattern

const wearingDress = current.dress !== "None";
document.getElementById('top').setAttribute('d', 
  wearingDress ? "" : paths.top[current.top]);

That condition ? valueIfTrue : valueIfFalse shape is called a ternary: a shorthand if/else in one line. Setting a shape's d to an empty string is a simple, reliable way to hide something without deleting it; her top and bottom choices stay remembered underneath, just not drawn.

transform: scale(): the one-line body swap

bodyGroup.setAttribute('transform', 
  `translate(130,0) scale(1.15,1) translate(-130,0)`);

A three-step sandwich: shift her whole body so her center lands at zero, stretch her horizontally, then shift her back to where she started. Doing the stretch around her true center is what keeps her proportional instead of sliding sideways as she resizes.

Layer order: the seating chart

<g id="bodyGroup">
  <rect class="skin"/>      <!-- legs -->
  <path id="shoesPath"/>    <!-- shoes -->
  <path class="skin"/>      <!-- torso -->
  <path id="bottom"/>       <!-- skirt/pants -->
  <path id="top"/>          <!-- shirt -->
  <path id="dress"/>        <!-- dress, if worn -->
</g>

Elements are painted in the order they appear in the code: later elements sit on top of earlier ones, like sheets of tracing paper stacked up. There's no "bring to front" button; if something's hidden, the only fix is moving it earlier or later in the markup. It's the single most common "why does this look wrong" bug in any layered 2D drawing.
